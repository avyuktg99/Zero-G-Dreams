A sleep schedule for the Moon.

Coming
soon

A lunar day is 29.5 Earth days long. The crew's body clock is still 24.

Sleep is usually the first item a schedule gives up. A long mission is planned in advance — operations, exercise, maintenance, meals — and when time runs short, the hours come out of sleep. On the ISS that has added up to roughly 6.1 hours a night against the 8.5 hours NASA's own standard recommends. One short night costs very little; a month of them slows reaction time and moves mistakes into the parts of a mission that can least afford them.

The Moon makes that harder. Light and dark arrive in two-week stretches, so sunrise and sunset stop working as cues for when to sleep; the schedule has to come from the mission plan instead. The plan is already crowded, because the work does not pause for night.

Hunch is the planner we are building to fix that. Give it a mission timeline and a crew, and it decides when each person should be asleep. From that one number it works backward through the rest of the day: when to start winding down, the last hour that exercise is allowed, the last coffee that will still be active at bedtime, and the wake time that keeps the next shift on schedule. Alongside the schedule it calculates alertness hour by hour, so a planner can see which operations land on a tired crew and move them before the mission flies.

Those numbers come from a nine-step model of how fatigue works: the sleep pressure that builds up since the last sleep period, the body clock it fights against, sleep lost to interruptions, and the debt carried into the next day. Every step is written out, and the chain is checked against 20 results from published NASA research. In our 28-sol simulation it brings mission fatigue down from 43.8 to 15.0 — about 66 percent. The planner runs as a single file on a plain laptop: no install, no account, no network, because the far side of the Moon has no connection to fall back on.
