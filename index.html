01 · The problem

A lunar day is 29.5 Earth days. The crew's clock is still 24.

Sleep is the first thing a mission schedule gives up. On the Moon the schedule has to come from the plan, not from sunrise — light and dark arrive in two-week stretches, and the habitat's lamps are the only thing telling a body what time it is.

02 · The gap

NASA schedules 8.5 hours. Astronauts sleep 6.09.

The 2.41 hours in between come out of schedule overruns, slow sleep onset and a dark, noisy station. And crews stop feeling the deficit before their performance stops showing it — measured in HERA, not assumed — so this is not something you can fix by asking people how tired they are.

03 · The tool

Give it a timeline. It gives back bedtimes.

Hunch takes the ops timeline and a crew, sets when each person should sleep, then works backwards through the day — wind-down, last exercise, last caffeine, wake time. Two views off one engine: the crew get one sol and one instruction, the planner gets the whole mission and a score.

04 · The engine

One function. No state, no clock, no network.

An array of days goes in, an array of scores comes out. Every input is an argument, which is why the same 212 lines run in Node for the checks and in the browser for the product — and why a reviewer can check the arithmetic by hand.

05 · The model

Nine steps, chained by dependency.

Where the body clock is, how far it can legally move, what is on board, how much bed the crew actually gets, where sleep fits around the ops — then what it all costs. Steps 1 to 7 to 8 to 9 is a real chain: the coffee residue cannot be known before the bedtime, and the bedtime cannot be known before the clock.

06 · The search

The score is a judge. A loop turns it into a plan.

Because a week re-scores in a fifth of a millisecond, the scheduler can shift one bedtime, score all 28 sols again, and keep the move if the number drops — about 40,000 scorings per run. It only moves sleep; ops stay fixed. Local search, so this is a legal plan far better than the baseline, not a proven optimum.

07 · The stack

No package manager. Nothing to install.

The tests and the product execute the same file, so the number on the slide cannot drift from the number in the app. There is no dependency tree, no lockfile and no version drift to audit on a flight computer — which matters more on a vehicle than on a laptop.

08 · Offline

It never tries to be online.

A laptop on the lunar surface cannot be assumed to have Node, a package manager, an internet connection, or permission to install anything. So the app writes a packet and hands it to the comms queue — 97 bytes per sol, small enough to ride along with telemetry already being sent. No network also means no attack surface.

Stated plainly: a schedule-design aid, not a fitness-for-duty call. Check-in persistence and flight-data calibration are the next steps.
